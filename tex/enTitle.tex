\en-abstract{
Conventional transport and positioning technologies serve mass production well, but emerging systems increasingly demand flexible motion, precise positioning, and clean operation at the same time. Mechanical contact and restricted motion make these requirements difficult to meet. A magnetically levitated planar motor (MLPM) removes contact between the stator and mover, eliminating contact friction and enabling planar motion with six-degree-of-freedom pose control. Realizing this capability requires coordinated magnetic force and torque modeling, pose measurement, current allocation, and feedback stabilization. The objective of this thesis is to develop and evaluate that integrated chain from a reference simulation to a physical prototype.
In the simulation stage, a reference model in MATLAB/Simulink, ROS~2, and Gazebo was connected to a fuzzy--PID controller and constrained current allocation. Six-coordinate step responses and pose-dependent actuation conditioning were evaluated. Manhattan-trajectory experiments showed how allowing more time for each segment reduced planar tracking error. A physical prototype with a two-dimensional Halbach mover and eight active coils was then constructed. Data from a geometry-specific Magpylib magnetic model were used to fit a fast surrogate for online allocation; on held-out data, its $R^2$ values ranged from $0.9546$ to $0.9742$ relative to that numerical model. Camera-based AprilTag pose estimation, the controller, and electrical drivers were integrated into a physical feedback loop. After staged, mechanically constrained planar, height, and orientation experiments, simultaneous control of all six coordinates was recorded for approximately $38.4\,\mathrm{s}$. Full-interval, vision-referenced root-mean-square errors were below $0.19\,\mathrm{mm}$ in both horizontal directions, $0.9916\,\mathrm{mm}$ in height, and below $0.30^\circ$ in each orientation angle. This experiment demonstrates six-coordinate feedback control of the physical prototype within a restricted operating region and underscores the need to design the system's components as a coordinated whole.
}
